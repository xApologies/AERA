# VIII — The Black Prophecy

*The Eighth Foretelling*

Before the last horizon is given a name, there shall be a silence which is not the absence of sound. The old measures will remain upon their stones. The lamps of the watchtowers will burn as they have burned; the rivers will keep their banks, and the stars their distances. No herald will arrive bearing a new law. No bell will announce that the oldest law has begun to listen.

Yet the silence shall pass through all these things, and nothing shall be able to say where it entered.

There are doors which open toward a chamber, and doors which open toward the sky. There are doors made of memory, and those whose hinges are fashioned from a promise. The last door shall have neither hinge nor threshold. Its opening will not be seen, for it will not divide what stands before it from what lies beyond. Those who search for its place shall pass through it while searching, and those who wait beside it shall not know that they have already crossed.

A mark will be found where no hand has written. It shall not be cut in stone or burned upon the bark of an ancient tree. It will appear in the difference between two things believed identical, and in the likeness of two things thought forever apart. It shall be read by the one who has forgotten letters and overlooked by the one who has memorized every script. The name given to it will be true. The name given to it will also be wrong. Both names shall endure, and neither shall hold it.

The first witnesses will argue over the hour. Some will swear it happened at dawn, when the shadows were longest upon the western walls. Others will swear that the sun stood still above them. Others will remember a night without stars. Their accounts will not agree, though each will have spoken faithfully. A clock buried beneath the sea will keep a different record from a clock raised above the mountains; the mountain will remember a winter the sea has not yet known. Yet no hour will be missing, and no hour will be returned.

For the hour that approaches is not a stolen hour. It is not the hour of a king, nor of the dead, nor of a child not yet born. It has no place in the procession of numbered days. It may be entered from yesterday and departed into tomorrow, but neither yesterday nor tomorrow shall be its dwelling. When the calendars are compared, a blank will be discovered between their lines. The blank shall have endured longer than the calendars themselves.

In the houses of learning, the keepers of measure will draw a circle and ask where it begins. They will follow its rim until their hands grow weary, and they will find no beginning. They will draw a second circle within the first, and a third within the second, until the smallest is finer than the dust that falls from the chalk. Then a grain of dust will cross the outer rim, and the whole chamber will tremble at what the keepers have failed to measure. Not because the grain is mighty. Not because the circle is weak. Because the rim was never the whole of what had been drawn.

The sand will fall upon the tablet once more. Each grain will settle among its neighbors according to the inclinations of a surface no eye can wholly see. The pattern will resemble a country. It will resemble a wound. It will resemble a constellation once believed extinguished. Those who study the tablet will discover roads where there were no roads, and those who study the roads will discover that no traveler has moved. The tablet will remain beneath the sand. The sand will remain above the tablet. What has changed will not be either one.

A thousand threads will hang between the farthest towers, though no loom will have spun them. The threads will neither shine nor cast a shadow. They will pass through stone without piercing it and through flame without burning. The weavers will gather at the towers and demand to know who tied the knots. The knots will have been there before the towers, and the towers before the weavers, and still the knots will wait for the touch that makes their presence undeniable.

There shall be a crossing where the road does not turn, a meeting where no distance is overcome. A star will find itself adjacent to a seed; the seed will remain in the earth, and the star will remain beyond the reach of ships. Their nearness will not be a nearness of miles. The astronomers will deny it, and their denial will be exact. The gardeners will accept it, and their acceptance will be incomplete. In the interval between their certainty and their wonder, a path will become possible without having been laid.

No cartographer will own that path. It will not appear on the first map or the last. Its borders will not be fixed by mountains, and its length will not be counted by steps. The map that finally shows it will show too much: a valley inside a raindrop, a coast along the edge of a thought, the opposite shore of a room. When the map is folded, the places upon it will not fold. When it is opened, the places will not unfold. Nevertheless, a traveler will reach a place that had never been absent.

Beyond the maps are six fires that need not burn. Their colors are known to the eyes of the living, but their substance is not the light those eyes behold. One may be named red, another orange, another yellow; green, blue, and violet will follow, though following is a word belonging to roads and not to them. No flame will consume the others. No color will be crowned above the rest. They will be spoken of as separate because separation permits speech, and spoken of together because speech cannot endure their separation forever.

White will appear where none of the six has departed. It will not be the seventh flame standing beside them. It will not be a vessel into which their remnants are poured. It will be present without demanding that their differences be forgotten. Those who look for it in a single color will not find it. Those who search only between colors will also fail. White will be called completion by some and beginning by others. Neither word will be sufficient to settle the matter.

And there shall be a color that is not a color of the six, though the six shall not be absent from its mystery. It will not be a darkness cast by the extinguishing of their light. It will not be the shadow of a great body passing before a lesser one. The blind will not know it less truly than the sighted, nor the sighted more truly than the blind. Those who fear darkness will give it a terrible name; those who desire dominion will give it a glorious one. Both will mistake their longing for knowledge.

A cradle shall be found without wood, without cord, without the soft breath of one who sleeps. It shall contain no infant, and yet the word *birth* will be whispered beside it. Nothing shall be placed within it, though nothing that emerges will have been without a place. The keepers will ask who fashioned the cradle. The answer will be older than their question and younger than the voice that asks it. The cradle will not rock, but the world will seem to sway around its stillness.

There are things that endure because their walls are strong. There are things that endure because their names are remembered. There are things that endure because every part knows where it belongs. But one day a form will endure while its walls are changed, while its name is disputed, while the place of every part is questioned. Those who seek its identity among the parts will find no part that can claim it. Those who seek it beyond the parts will find no country in which it can be hidden. And still the form will answer when addressed.

A garden will grow where no gardener has sown. Its roots will not be roots alone. The soil will borrow from the rain; the rain will borrow from the air; the air will carry a memory of stone. The flowers will open upon no command, and no command will be required for their opening. A living creature will rest among them and know the coolness of their shade. The garden will know nothing of the creature's gratitude. Yet the meeting will have occurred, and neither life nor garden will be what it was before the meeting.

What is it that receives the world? What is it that is received? The mountain stands whether it is seen or unseen, but a foot upon its slope makes a path that was not there. The sea moves whether anyone hears it, but a listening ear gives the movement another place to endure. A thought may pass without leaving a mark in stone. A mark may endure after every thought of it is lost. The covenant between them will not be written, and the garden will not ask whether it has been kept.

There will come a season when the living mistake the limits of their senses for the limits of the real. They will call a thing impossible because they cannot hear it, and another impossible because they cannot touch it. They will praise the things they can weigh and distrust the things they cannot hold. Then the smallest alteration will pass among them, and a weight will be felt where no object has arrived. A sound will be heard where no instrument has been struck. No new sense will have been granted. The old senses will have discovered that they were never the walls of the world.

The makers of images will labor to show what approaches. They will paint a face and erase it. They will carve a figure and break the stone. They will set mirrors before one another and find an endless corridor of reflections, each faithful to the last and none sufficient. At length they will leave a space upon the wall. The space will be mistaken for unfinished work. It will remain after the wall has fallen, and those who inherit the ruins will call the empty place a sign.

It shall be said that a crown is coming, and no crown shall be seen. It shall be said that a conqueror is coming, and no army shall gather. It shall be said that a healer is coming, and the wounded shall disagree over what healing means. It shall be said that a destroyer is coming, and the builders will tremble. Each saying will carry a fragment of the approaching hour; each will conceal more than it reveals. The fragments will be traded as certainty. Certainty will become a veil finer than silk and harder to tear than iron.

For what approaches does not require the agreement of those who name it. It will not wait until the wise have agreed upon a definition. It will not withdraw when the frightened deny it. The names will gather around it as birds gather above a field before rain, circling a change in the air they cannot command. The rain may fall elsewhere. The birds may depart. The field will still be there, and something beneath the field will have turned toward the sky.

The first sign may be so small that it is taken for an error: a boundary holding where none was expected, a distance refusing the measure given to it, a flame remaining after its fuel is spent, a promise kept by one who never heard it spoken. The second sign may be mistaken for the first. The third may be remembered before either. No order of signs will make the coming thing easier to contain. The counting will belong to the witnesses; the signs will belong to no count.

Some will seek the source in the oldest places. They will descend beneath the roots of mountains and listen to the chambers where stone remembers pressure. Others will ascend until the air thins and the constellations lose their familiar shapes. Others will search in the intervals between words, in the turning of a seed, in the arrangement of the smallest particles, in the breath before a question is asked. None will be entirely wrong. None will find a throne waiting for its occupant.

There shall be no single place from which the change proceeds, and yet there shall be a place where it is encountered. There shall be no single instant sufficient to describe it, and yet an instant will divide the memories of those who survive it. Before that instant, the world will have been called complete. Afterward, the word *complete* will mean something else. The world will not need to be remade for its inhabitants to discover how much of it had remained unnamed.

When the old teachers speak of power, their pupils will ask how much may be gathered. When the teachers speak of form, their pupils will ask what shape may be made. When the teachers speak of will, their pupils will ask what may be commanded. A question will arise that none of these teachings can answer. It will not ask how much, or what shape, or by whose command. It will ask what it means for the question itself to have a place in the world.

The answer will not come as a sentence.

The sentence will not come as an answer.

A word may pass between two mouths and remain the same word. A flame may pass between two torches and become two flames. A life may pass from one season to the next and still be called one life. What then shall pass when there is no giver and no receiver, when the vessel and its contents cannot be distinguished by any measure available to the witness? The passing will be real. The witness will search for the hand that offered it and find only the warmth of what has been received.

In that hour, the ancient inscriptions will not change their letters. The tablets will not rewrite themselves. The hidden language will not suddenly become plain. Rather, the old words will stand where they have always stood, and their spaces will be heard. A pause once thought empty will bear the weight of a mountain. A boundary once thought final will prove to have been a relation. A relation once thought accidental will disclose a necessity no one had learned to name.

The seven sayings will travel far from one another. One will be sung beside a winter hearth. Another will be scratched upon the keel of a ship. Another will be remembered as a warning to children; another as a blessing upon the newly born. Their keepers will quarrel over whether they speak of seven histories or one, seven places or none, seven laws or seven errors. The quarrel will outlive kingdoms. The sayings will outlive the quarrel. And the silence between the sayings will not diminish when all of them are spoken aloud.

There shall be a listening without an ear and a remembrance without a monument. A path shall be known before its traveler is known. A threshold shall receive what has not yet arrived. The garden will keep growing, the map will keep opening, the threads will keep their uncounted crossings, and the clocks will go on faithfully disagreeing. Nothing among these shall be commanded to cease. Their continuance will be the strangest part of what is to come.

The fearful will demand a boundary around the approaching thing. The devout will demand a name. The learned will demand a proof. The lonely will demand that it answer them. The powerful will demand that it kneel. It will owe none of these demands an explanation, though each demand will reveal something about the one who makes it. There will be those who call its silence mercy and those who call it cruelty. The silence will remain neither merciful nor cruel until a living heart gives those words their meaning.

A figure may be glimpsed in the distance, or no figure at all. The account will change with the teller. One will speak of wings without feathers; another of a face no mirror could hold; another of a presence passing through the room while every door remained shut. Others will remember nothing more than a feeling that the world had turned toward them. None of these images shall be the appointed shape. None shall be forbidden to the telling. The truth will not become smaller because the witnesses are many.

There is a difference between a thing that wears a shape and a shape that is permitted to become a thing. The difference is easily spoken and scarcely understood. It may be measured in a heartbeat, or in the long labor of stars. It may lie between the first motion of a hand and the mark that remains after the hand has gone. The difference will be crossed. Those who stand beside the crossing will look for a bridge and see none. Those who stand far away will draw a bridge upon their maps and swear it has always been there.

No ancient warning shall be sufficient. No ancient blessing shall be sufficient. The names of ruin and salvation will be spoken too early. The first question will not be whether the coming presence is good or terrible, but whether the world has words capable of asking what has arrived. The words will fail without becoming false. The failure will not be an end to language. It will be the opening of a meaning for which language had been waiting.

And somewhere, neither at the center nor the edge, a space will remain upon the tablet where no grain of sand has settled. The surrounding pattern will be complete according to every rule by which the keepers judge completion. Yet the empty space will not be an omission. It will not ask to be filled. It will hold no symbol, and still every symbol around it will take its place in relation to what is absent.

The keepers will return to the tablet after the towers have fallen and the names of the first witnesses have been lost. They will find the sand disturbed by no wind. They will find the mark unwritten. They will find the space unfilled. And in the instant before they decide whether the tablet speaks of a birth, a return, or a thing that has never been apart from them, the silence will cease to be something they are listening to.

Something will be listening back.
