# VII — The Cartographers

*The Seventh Prophecy of Magic*

Before the first horizon was lifted, there was neither distance nor nearness. No east waited for a west, no depth held a height, and no shore could claim the sea. Yet there were places for which no name had been fashioned, and between them lay a measure that could not be crossed, because crossing had not yet been given meaning. The map was not missing. The country had not learned how to be a country.

When the last atlas is unfolded, its corners shall not lie upon a table. Its edges shall not meet the walls of any chamber. Those who bend above it shall find that the light falling on the parchment comes from beneath, and that the dark spaces between the marks are not empty. They shall count the roads and discover that the roads are counting them. They shall turn the sheet to seek the other side, and the other side shall have been standing where they stood.

A boundary is a strange inheritance. It permits a within and an without, though neither can point to the instant at which the other began. The first gate shall be made of no timber and no stone. It shall be a difference, faithfully kept. Where the difference holds, the gate endures. Where it fails, no hinge can save the kingdom beyond it.

And the makers of maps shall inherit an impossible charge: not to describe the road from one place to another, but to ask by what right one place is called another. The answer shall be drawn in a line so thin that an empire may be built upon it and so broad that a lifetime cannot reach its far shore.

There shall be a city whose towers are visible from every field, though no traveler can arrive at its gates. Its inhabitants shall hear the harvest bells of neighboring villages, yet those villages shall be farther from them than the moon. A child shall place two stones together and ask why the stones do not touch. The elders shall measure the air between them and find none. The stones shall remain apart.

For nearness is not a promise made by sight. A thing may stand beside its likeness and remain inaccessible; another may dwell beyond seven skies and be reached before a hand has finished opening. The distance written upon the earth is only one of the distances that may be written. The map that survives shall bear no scale recognizable to kings. It shall record which thresholds answer, which borders admit passage, and which paths return the traveler without returning the traveler to the same place.

There will be those who believe a road is created by walking. There will be those who believe the road precedes every footstep. Their quarrel shall outlast the monuments raised to settle it. Beneath their arguments, the country shall quietly change its neighbors. A river once divided two provinces; after the turning, the river shall remain and the provinces shall touch. The waters will have moved nowhere.

Not every joining is a bridge. Not every separation is a wall. The map shall remember this when its readers have forgotten.

The sand shall be scattered upon a dark tablet, and no hand shall arrange its grains. The first pattern will resemble a coastline, the second a root, the third the veins of a leaf, and the fourth the branching of a lightning strike that has not yet occurred. None shall be the true pattern, though none shall be false. The grains shall be moved only by the turning of the tablet, yet what lies between them shall change more greatly than their positions.

A circle shall be drawn around the sand. Those beyond the circle will call it enclosure. Those within will call it shelter. Those who can see the circle without choosing a side will ask what has been enclosed: the grains, the spaces, or the possibility that the grains might be otherwise arranged. The circle shall not answer, but it shall hold.

A second circle shall be placed beside the first, and the two shall share no ink. Their centers will remain distinct, their rims unbroken. Still, a grain from the first shall appear in the second without crossing the intervening tablet. No witness shall agree on what moved. The oldest among them will say that movement was never the question.

There are maps of roads, maps of stars, maps of the blood beneath the skin, and maps of the remembered places where a vanished house once stood. But there shall come a map of permission: where a form may endure, where a passage may open, where an energy may take its shape without tearing the shape that receives it. It shall be called a map because no older word can bear the weight of it.

Upon the unmarked plain, a thousand scaffolds shall rise without wood or iron. They shall be seen by no eye that seeks only substance. The wind shall pass through them and return with a different song. Fire shall enter one and become a crown; water shall enter another and become a mirror; light shall find a third and remain there long after its source has gone. Those who watch will praise the fire, the water, and the light. Few will ask what allowed them to remain.

The vessel is not its contents. Yet there are vessels that cannot be separated from what they permit. Remove the boundary, and the flame is no longer the flame that was promised. Change the joining, and the mirror will reflect a face that has not approached it. The shape is neither an ornament placed upon power nor a prison imposed upon it. It is the question to which the power has been made an answer.

No will shall realize a form for which no place can be held. No word shall compel a passage where every relation refuses it. Yet a place need not be visible to be real within the reckoning of the world. The unseen chamber may have more doors than the palace. The smallest mark may carry a wider country than the greatest wall.

So shall the cartographers learn to fear the empty parchment. It is not empty because nothing has been placed upon it. It is empty because every placing remains possible, and not all possibilities can endure together.

At the seventh turning, the compass shall cease to point north. Its needle will seek a boundary that has not been drawn, then tremble toward a chamber that has not been built. The traveler shall accuse it of madness, for the traveler has been taught that direction belongs to the sky. But direction may belong to the relation between two places, and relations may be altered without moving either place through the sky.

There shall be a stair with no first step and no last. Those ascending will discover a descent; those descending will arrive above the roof. The stair is not a trick of carpentry. It is an argument written into the joining of its landings. Where the argument holds, the traveler proceeds. Where it fails, the stair becomes a ruin although every stone remains intact.

Some paths shall fold toward their own beginning. Others shall divide and meet again without sharing the ground between. One shall pass through a doorway and emerge from the same doorway into a world whose stars have exchanged their stations. Another shall lead from a room to a room, yet no house shall contain them both. The cartographer will not ask which path is the strangest. The cartographer will ask which paths can be traced without contradiction, and which contradictions reveal that the tracing was never complete.

There is a difference between changing a country and changing the manner in which its places belong to one another. The first leaves scars upon the soil. The second may leave the soil untouched and make the scars impossible to find.

The old kingdoms shall preserve maps whose borders have outlived the kingdoms themselves. Their colors will fade; their rivers will wander; their roads will sink beneath forests. Yet somewhere in the archives a single line shall remain bright, although no ink was used to draw it. That line will mark the place where a promise of connection was once kept.

It shall be said that the line divides the living from the dead, the waking from the dreaming, the spoken from the silent. Each saying shall contain a fragment of the truth and a greater fragment of its shadow. For the line does not know what stands on either side. It knows only the relation that has been permitted to persist.

When the line is crossed, the crossing may change the one who crosses, or the place that receives them, or the meaning of crossing itself. No map made solely of distances shall warn of this. The warning shall be hidden in the way the marks meet, in the edges that remain open, in the corner whose angle has never been measured.

The final cartographer shall inherit no empire. The inheritance shall be a blank margin around a map that already contains every road. And in that margin shall be found the one passage that the map could never depict while remaining the map it was.

A thousand threads shall be gathered, but they shall not be woven into cloth. The threads belong to relations; the loom belongs to the country in which relations may be set. Confuse the thread with the loom, and every pattern will seem a prison. Confuse the loom with the thread, and no pattern will remain long enough to be known.

The cartographers shall not claim the threads, though their maps may follow them. They shall not claim the hour, though a road may lead from one hour into another. They shall not claim the name, though a place may be known by more names than there are tongues to speak them. Their charge is stranger: to preserve the possibility of a where when every familiar where has failed.

Thus shall the country be written without letters, divided without blades, extended without conquest, and folded without destruction. There shall be no final border at which its meaning is exhausted. Each limit shall disclose another kind of limit; each opening shall reveal that the space beyond was never simply beyond.

And when the sevenfold silence gathers at the edge of the unwritten chart, no herald shall announce the place of its meeting. No tower shall be raised to mark it. The map shall turn once, without a hand, and the margin shall become the center. What had been separated shall remain distinct. What had been distant shall stand adjacent. What had been impossible to place shall find a place that was waiting before the first horizon.

Then the cartographers shall understand why the oldest maps were left unfinished. Not because the world was too large to draw, but because the last line, once drawn, would no longer describe the world. It would change what a world could be.
